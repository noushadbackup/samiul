\section*{Abstract}
Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative condition that causes memory loss and cognitive decline, and early detection is essential to improve patient care and treatment planning. Structural MRI reliably captures the brain changes linked to Alzheimer's, yet many reported deep-learning systems inflate their accuracy by splitting the data at the level of individual 2D slices, which lets scans from the same patient appear in both training and testing. This work develops and benchmarks a family of fully three-dimensional models for Alzheimer's classification on the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset (1{,}132 T1-weighted MPRAGE volumes from 395 subjects), under a strict \emph{subject-level}, leakage-free evaluation protocol with five-fold cross-validation. Two clinically meaningful tasks are studied: the binary task of separating cognitively normal (CN) subjects from Alzheimer's (AD) subjects, and the harder three-class task of CN vs.\ mild cognitive impairment (MCI) vs.\ AD. Five architectures are compared on an identical pipeline and split: a 3D DenseNet-121 CNN, a MedicalNet-pretrained 3D ResNet, a 3D Vision Transformer (ViT), a Swin Transformer encoder, and a hybrid that fuses a 3D CNN with a 3D ViT to combine local anatomical detail with global contextual reasoning. The hybrid CNN--ViT delivers the best binary result at $82.2\% \pm 5.7$ accuracy (macro-F1 $0.80$), marginally ahead of the from-scratch DenseNet ($81.0\%$) and well ahead of the pretrained ResNet ($69.8\%$); on the three-class task the honest ceiling is $\sim\!57.7\%$ (Swin), with MCI remaining the dominant source of confusion, and a from-scratch ViT collapsing to near-chance --- itself a useful negative result about data-hungry transformers on small medical datasets. Model decisions are examined with a suite of 3D explainability methods (Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, and attention rollout), whose importance maps concentrate on medial-temporal and ventricular regions consistent with known AD pathology. The contribution of this work is not a headline-chasing accuracy number but a rigorous, reproducible, leakage-free comparison of single versus hybrid 3D architectures for Alzheimer's classification, together with an interpretability analysis that grounds the predictions in plausible neuroanatomy.
