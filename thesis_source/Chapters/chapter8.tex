
This chapter presents the concluding remarks of the study and outlines directions for future work. It summarizes the objectives, methodological contributions, and experimental findings of the Alzheimer's detection framework, reflects on what the results actually show, acknowledges the limitations encountered, and proposes enhancements to guide further research.
\section{Summary of the Work}
This research developed and benchmarked a family of fully three-dimensional deep-learning models for Alzheimer's Disease classification from structural MRI. Working on the ADNI dataset (1{,}132 T1-weighted volumes from 395 subjects), five architectures—a 3D DenseNet CNN, a MedicalNet-pretrained 3D ResNet, a 3D Vision Transformer, a Swin Transformer, and the proposed hybrid 3D CNN–ViT—were trained on an identical pipeline and compared under a strict subject-level five-fold protocol. Two tasks were studied: binary CN-vs-AD and three-class CN/MCI/AD. The design deliberately prioritized an \emph{honest}, leakage-free comparison over a headline accuracy number, and integrated 3D Grad-CAM and related attribution methods for interpretability. The study finds that combining local CNN features with global transformer context gives the highest binary mean, but no improvement over a strong standalone CNN could be detected statistically.

\section{Key Findings and Contributions}
The experiments produced several clear findings. The proposed \textbf{hybrid 3D CNN–ViT had the highest binary CN-vs-AD mean ($82.2\%$ accuracy, macro-F1 $0.80$)}, but it was statistically indistinguishable from a strong from-scratch 3D DenseNet ($81.0\%$; paired fold-wise $p\approx0.54$, three of five folds won), so no benefit from hybridisation could be demonstrated. Both models clearly exceed the $65.4\%$ majority-class baseline. On the three-class task the best result was $57.7\%$ (Swin), only about five points above the $52.2\%$ majority-class baseline, with MCI, the transitional stage, the dominant source of confusion; the hybrid was not evaluated on this task. Two instructive negative results emerged. The MedicalNet-\emph{pretrained} ResNet underperformed the from-scratch CNN and was highly unstable across folds, plausibly because of an early-stopping artifact that remains to be tested. The from-scratch 3D ViT degenerated to never predicting the majority class, which is consistent with data-hungry transformers needing large-scale pretraining, possibly compounded by stacked imbalance corrections.

The contributions of this study are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A rigorous, leakage-free benchmark.} A single-source ADNI cohort evaluated with subject-level five-fold cross-validation and an identical pipeline across all models, giving a genuinely fair single-vs-hybrid comparison—in contrast to the slice-level, leakage-prone evaluations that dominate the literature~\cite{wen2020convolutional,yagis2021effect}.
    \item \textbf{The hybrid 3D CNN–ViT design and its honest assessment.} A CNN branch for local structure and a ViT branch for long-range context, fused by late concatenation, evaluated against single-model baselines with a paired statistical comparison --- which shows that the gain over a strong 3D CNN is not detectable at this data scale.
    \item \textbf{An end-to-end 3D explainability pipeline and a frank assessment of it.} Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, Score-CAM, Integrated Gradients, SmoothGrad, occlusion, LIME, and attention rollout are implemented for 3D volumes. The analysis shows that, without skull-stripping and registration, the methods disagree and partly attend to non-brain tissue, and it identifies the steps needed before such maps can support anatomical claims.
    \item \textbf{A reproducible, config-driven codebase} that regenerates the curated labels, subject-level splits, all model runs, and every figure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations of the Study}
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort, though standard, is small (395 subjects; only 80 MCI), which caps three-class performance and makes transformer training difficult. The single-cohort ADNI focus (multi-site, but one study protocol) keeps the labels and protocol clean; a first attempt at external validation on independent hospital scans (Section~\ref{sec:extval}) was constrained by a severe modality/domain shift and is reported below as a preliminary result rather than a demonstration of transfer. The 16\,GB GPU budget constrained input resolution ($96^3$–$128^3$) and batch size, and limited the number of experiments: standalone ViT and Swin runs on the binary task and a CNN–ViT run on the three-class task are missing, every result comes from a single seed, and the binary CNN's diagnostic figures cover only three of the five folds because checkpoints were not retained. The pretrained-ResNet result may be confounded by an early-stopping artifact, and AD subjects are older than CN subjects, so age may contribute to the signal. Because the ADNI volumes are not skull-stripped or atlas-registered, the explainability maps report importance over coarse spatial regions, disagree across methods, partly fall on non-brain tissue, and were not computed for the proposed hybrid. These limitations are documented here rather than hidden.

\section{Preliminary External Validation on Independent Hospital Data}\label{sec:extval}
To probe generalization beyond ADNI, the trained models were applied to a small set of real clinical MRI scans obtained from a collaborating diagnostic center, \textbf{Comfort Diagnostic Center}. Weak diagnostic labels for these scans were derived from routine radiology reports under the guidance of a collaborating clinician, \textbf{Dr.\ Ziaur Rahman} (Physician \& Surgeon); the labels are termed \emph{weak} because they come from clinical reports rather than a formal research diagnostic protocol. This exercise is reported transparently as a \emph{preliminary} pilot test, not as a headline result. Table~\ref{tab:extval} summarises the outcome across models.

\textbf{Label validity.} Mild cognitive impairment is a clinical diagnosis based on cognitive assessment; it cannot be established from a structural radiology report alone. The CN and MCI labels used here are therefore \emph{unvalidated} proxies, not merely weak labels, and the results below must be read with that in mind.

\noindent{\footnotesize $^{*}$The apparently high 0.94 binary AUC is \textbf{age-confounded} and must \emph{not} be read as evidence of transfer (see below).}

The attempt surfaced a severe \textbf{domain shift}. Whereas the models were trained on 3\,T, 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE volumes at $\sim$1\,mm isotropic resolution, the hospital scans were 1.5\,T, 2D T1-FLAIR acquisitions of roughly 20 slices at 5--6\,mm slice thickness (about $12\times$ anisotropic) --- a fundamentally different modality and resolution regime. Only 8 studies were usable (4 CN, 4 MCI; no AD cases were available), and three-class accuracy fell to chance ($\le$3/8 for every model). The seemingly high binary CN-vs-MCI AUC of 0.94 is \emph{not} evidence of transfer: the CN and MCI subgroups are strongly \textbf{age-confounded} (mean age $\sim$35\,years for CN vs.\ $\sim$68\,years for MCI), so the model is largely tracking age-related atrophy rather than disease, and the sample is far too small for a reliable estimate. The one mild positive is that the binary model assigned a low AD probability ($<0.17$) to all four CN subjects --- it did not raise false AD alarms --- though with no AD cases present, sensitivity could not be assessed. With only eight subjects every figure in Table~\ref{tab:extval} is extremely uncertain: for example, the best CN-vs-MCI result of 6/8 has a 95\% Wilson confidence interval of roughly 41--93\%, and the ViT's AUC of $0.12$ simply reflects an inverted ranking on eight cases.

The honest reading is that this preliminary test does \emph{not} demonstrate transfer; rather, it shows how sharply performance degrades when the acquisition protocol differs from the training distribution, while establishing a realistic data-sharing pathway with the hospital. A meaningful external validation requires 3\,T, 3D volumetric T1-weighted scans with confirmed, research-grade labels including AD cases, which is set out as future work below.

\section{Recommendations and Future Work}
Future work follows directly from these limitations. First, complete the experimental grid --- standalone ViT and Swin on the binary task and the CNN--ViT hybrid on the three-class task --- repeat the cross-validation with several seeds, and compare models with paired statistical tests. Re-run the pretrained ResNet with a minimum-epoch guard before early stopping, and the three-class ViT with a single imbalance correction, to test the two explanations proposed in Chapter~\ref{cha:results}. Add an age-only (or age-and-sex) logistic-regression baseline to measure how much of the signal age alone explains, and regenerate all diagnostic figures from all five folds. Second, run the skull-stripping (e.g.\ SynthStrip or HD-BET) and MNI-registration ablations, initialise the Swin encoder from self-supervised pretrained weights, and recompute the explainability maps for the proposed CNN--ViT, summing importance over atlas ROIs (e.g.\ the hippocampus) and adding sanity checks such as model-randomisation tests and deletion curves. These steps are needed before RQ2 can be answered. Third, extend the external validation begun here: collect 3\,T, 3D T1-weighted scans with research-grade labels (including AD cases) from the collaborating hospital, and additionally validate on public independent datasets (e.g., MIRIAD or OASIS), to measure genuine cross-site generalization free of the modality mismatch and age confound described above. Finally, the hybrid can be extended with a tuned cross-attention fusion and with multimodal inputs (PET, cognitive scores) to improve the difficult CN/MCI/AD boundary, and evaluated in a realistic clinical workflow. Together these steps would turn a rigorous benchmark into a stronger, externally validated diagnostic-support tool.
